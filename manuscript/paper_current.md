Silicon Sampling Captures Country-Level Assumptions, Not Individual Attitudes:
Cross-National Evidence from the European Social Survey
Abstract
Silicon sampling uses large language models (LLMs) to simulate survey respondents. Whether it recovers cross-national variation, and why, is unresolved. This study evaluates it against European Social Survey Round 11 (30 countries, 42 items) with two major open-weight LLMs, two prompt framings, and a backstory-conditioning experiment. Aggregate recovery is moderate and uneven across items. Adding the country name to a three-variable demographic backstory raises the median country-level correlation from −0.03 to 0.52. No richer profile surpasses it. Swapping in a wrong country name leaves the respondents’ own country less well recovered than giving no country name at all. The model uses the country as a group-level prior and largely ignores respondent detail. Individual-level recovery is negligible everywhere and unrelated to aggregate recovery across countries. Naming the two endpoints of the response scale in words removes the item-level inversions and roughly triples individual recovery. A leave-one-out regional average, using no model at all, outperforms silicon sampling on both rank and level accuracy. Silicon sampling can support exploratory aggregate comparison after item-level validation. It does not support individual-level or distributional inference.
Keywords: silicon sampling, large language models, cross-national variation, survey methodology
1 Introduction
Cross-national surveys are indispensable to the social sciences. However, they are increasingly difficult to sustain, as costs rise and response rates decline (Curtin et al., 2005; Keeter et al., 2017). Against this backdrop, large language models (LLMs) have emerged as a potential source of synthetic survey data. Using silicon sampling, an LLM conditioned on a respondent’s demographic profile is intended to generate responses approximating those of a comparable human (Argyle et al., 2023). This offers a low-cost and real-time alternative to survey data collection. The same generative capabilities, however, also threaten survey integrity from the other direction: AI agents can now produce human-passing responses that evade standard quality checks (Westwood, 2025).
Early evaluations found that model responses track human ones across several domains. Argyle et al. (2023) report that GPT-3, when conditioned on sociodemographic backstories from the American National Election Studies, produces silicon samples whose correlational patterns mirror those of human respondents across multiple attitudinal domains. Dillion et al. (2023) likewise document close correspondence in the moral domain, reporting that model judgments of the wrongness of everyday actions closely correlate with human ratings. In experimental settings, Ashokkumar et al. (2026) show that GPT-4 can predict treatment effects in 70 nationally representative U.S. survey experiments.
Later work found compressed distributions and misrepresented groups. Bisbee et al. (2024) show that synthetic responses exhibit compressed variance, prompt sensitivity, and regression coefficients that diverge from human data. Dominguez-Olmedo et al. (2024) find strong ordering and labeling effects and near-uniform adjusted response distributions. Wang et al. (2025) document flattened within-group heterogeneity and misrepresentation of marginalized groups. Cross-nationally, model outputs remain closer to Western and English-speaking populations (Durmus et al., 2023; Tao et al., 2024).
Four gaps constrain the existing silicon-sampling literature. First, most evaluations remain centered on the United States. Available cross-national studies cover few countries or items. Second, many studies examine a narrow attitudinal domain, leaving item-level heterogeneity poorly characterized. Third, existing cross-national work shows that changing a country cue changes model responses (Qu & Wang, 2024). However, it does not compare otherwise identical sparse profiles with and without the country label. It also does not assess subsequent profile enrichment across a broad survey battery. Fourth, individual-level recovery is generally summarized across all respondents, leaving open whether it varies across countries and whether its geographic pattern aligns with aggregate recovery.
Together, the four gaps leave the central question open: why does aggregate recovery occur where it does? Two things are at issue: what supplies the cross-national signal, and what decides its direction. A cross-national design can separate two candidate sources of aggregate recovery that single-country studies cannot compare: a country label may activate country-level associations, while compositional differences across national samples may also generate cross-national variation, the mechanism demographic conditioning assumes (Argyle et al., 2023). Using European Social Survey (ESS) Round 11 data from 30 countries and 42 items, this paper evaluates aggregate and individual recovery and estimates the incremental contribution of the country label against the rest of the respondent profile.
To address these gaps, this study puts three questions to one design, in four steps. The first characterizes aggregate recovery item by item across 30 countries and 42 items, which fixes the pattern to be explained. The second isolates the country label’s contribution from demographic composition and from profile enrichment in a backstory-conditioning experiment. The third crosses the country label with the response format in a 2 × 2, turning the interpretation of the item inversions into a test. The fourth estimates within-country individual recovery for each country and tests its geographic pattern against the aggregate one, which bounds what the pattern delivers. Throughout, recovery is judged against model-free comparators.
Overall, the models treat the country name as a group-level prior. The label carries most of the aggregate signal. Enriching the profile beyond it adds no consistent gain. The response format decides whether the model ranks countries the right way round or backwards. None of it amounts to individual-level recovery. The study contributes on three fronts. The empirical contribution is a joint evaluation of item-level country rankings, within-country individual recovery, and the label’s incremental contribution on the same matched respondents. The methodological one is the experimental identification of that prior and of the format that decides which way the ordering runs. The practical one is a benchmark against model-free alternatives, the comparison a prospective user needs.
2 Related Literature and Research Questions
2.1 Cross-national evaluation of silicon sampling
Evaluating silicon sampling cross-nationally matters because comparative social science seeks to explain cross-national differences in public attitudes. For example, cross-national differences in political trust and system support are central to comparative accounts of civic culture and democratic legitimacy (Norris, 2011). Differences in political values underpin theories of modernization and cultural change (Inglehart & Welzel, 2005; Dalton & Welzel, 2014). Subjective well-being likewise varies systematically with national economic, institutional, and cultural conditions (Diener et al., 1995). All three domains appear in the 42-item battery evaluated below. An LLM-based substitute must recover country means and structured cross-national variation without introducing response-scale artifacts.
Existing cross-national evidence remains largely within one or a few countries. Qu and Wang (2024) compare one environmental item across six World Values Survey countries and a political item in the United States and find higher agreement in Western, English-speaking, and developed settings. Cao et al. (2025) use two global surveys and find that specialization improves distributional correspondence, although performance remains weaker on unseen questions and output diversity does not consistently track accuracy. Pataranutaporn et al. (2025) likewise find that individual well-being predictions are least accurate in underrepresented countries, but examine well-being only.
Broader cross-national benchmarks show the same unevenness. Durmus et al. (2023) find that default model responses are closer to some national distributions than others and that country prompting shifts responses without ensuring fidelity. Tao et al. (2024) compare five GPT models with values data from 107 countries and territories. Default outputs align most closely with English-speaking and Protestant European profiles, while cultural prompting improves correspondence for most countries but does not eliminate cross-country error. Together, these studies benchmark broad correspondence, but none jointly evaluates item-level country rankings, within-country individual recovery, and the incremental contribution of the country cue in a matched respondent-level design.
The literature supports two expectations. First, recovery should be uneven across items, and part of that unevenness should follow how the response scale is presented, because option format shifts model answers independently of content (Section 2.2). Second, a pooled correlation that mixes items of different scale lengths should overstate recovery, because much of the covariance it captures falls between items and not between countries.
RQ1. How accurately does silicon sampling recover public attitudes at the aggregate level, and how far does that accuracy vary from item to item?
2.2 Cultural bias and the country cue in LLM responses
Before any country is named, model outputs already lean toward some populations. Cultural bias here means systematic differences in whose values and associations those default outputs reproduce. Internet-scale training data and model-development choices draw unevenly on languages and regions, making English-language and Western perspectives especially salient (Bender et al., 2021; Tao et al., 2024). A model can therefore answer fluently across languages while still defaulting to associations that are not representative of the target population. Naous et al. (2024), for example, find that multilingual and Arabic models favor Western over Arab cultural entities even in Arabic-language tasks. Uneven correspondence also appears within countries: Santurkar et al. (2023) find the closest correspondence to wealthier, educated, and politically liberal Americans. Model outputs are not a culturally neutral baseline.
Naming a country moves the output, though not necessarily toward the population named. In human judgment, national-character ratings can be consistent across raters yet fail to correspond to population mean traits (Terracciano et al., 2005). This illustrates that a country cue may activate a stereotype instead of recovering the distribution it names. Language models likewise attach systematic valence to national demonyms, with more negative sentiment for countries with fewer internet users and lower economic status (Narayanan Venkit et al., 2023). In public-opinion simulation, changing the simulated country shifts model responses (Qu & Wang, 2024). Yet the resulting correspondence is ambiguous: agreement with national averages may reflect calibrated country knowledge, stereotypic associations, or both.
The response format can create cross-national differences by itself. Format here means how the answer scale is presented: numbers alone, or numbers whose endpoints are named in words. Reversed and negated items are a known source of misresponse in human surveys, where a minority of respondents answer them as though the direction had not changed (Weijters & Baumgartner, 2012). Language models show an analogous sensitivity to option format and position (Zheng et al., 2024), and the measured political position of a model can change substantially with the format in which the question is posed (Röttger et al., 2024). A numeric range without verbal endpoints leaves the scale direction to be inferred, and format effects can dominate synthetic distributions (Dominguez-Olmedo et al., 2024). Where numbers carry different meanings for human and model, the comparison fails the measurement-equivalence condition (King et al., 2004; Davidov et al., 2014). Cue and format can both generate cross-national structure. Crossing them separates the two.
Both the country label and the scale presentation are plausible sources of aggregate signal. This study therefore tests whether the country label adds recovery beyond demographic composition and whether richer profiles add further gains. The two readings of the label predict two different patterns. If the label activates calibrated knowledge that richer detail refines, recovery should rise with each level of enrichment. If it activates a group-level prior, the label should produce one large step and further detail should add little. A label account also predicts that the cue is specific to the country named: attaching the wrong country’s name should pull recovery toward the named country and away from the respondents’ real one. Format predicts something different. If the numeric scale is read in the wrong direction on reverse-coded items, the same country knowledge will rank countries backwards on those items and correctly elsewhere, and supplying verbal endpoints should remove the inversion without touching the model’s knowledge.
RQ2. Which part of the prompt produces the aggregate signal, and why does the model rank countries backwards on some items?
2.3 Individual-level simulation and its relation to aggregate recovery
Individual-level simulation sets a stricter standard than reproducing group means: model responses must preserve covariation between profile attributes and attitudes within populations. Recent work therefore evaluates aggregate, distributional, and individual recovery separately (Li et al., 2025; Miranda & Balbi, 2025). Although Argyle et al. (2023) report encouraging correlational structure in U.S. silicon samples, subsequent work identifies weak distributional and individual recovery. Bisbee et al. (2024) document compressed variance and unstable regression coefficients. Dominguez-Olmedo et al. (2024) show that response-format effects can dominate the resulting distributions. Wang et al. (2025) find that identity prompting flattens within-group heterogeneity and can reproduce out-group portrayals, not in-group responses. Even minimal identity cues such as dialect can shift model judgments (Hofmann et al., 2024). Persona responsiveness is not equivalent to accurate within-population differentiation.
Being the person and predicting the person are two different requests. Answering in persona speaks from the profile. Predicting how the person would answer reasons about it. If individual recovery depends on mapping attributes to attitudes, the two need not perform alike. The contrast also bears on the source question: a group-level prior gives the model nothing to gain from speaking as the person, so under that account the two framings should not differ. The design crosses model with first- and third-person framing, and RQ3 tests it.
Richer conditioning rescues individual recovery only when it is far richer than a demographic profile. On one side, agents grounded in interview or survey self-reports reproduce respondents’ answers at 82–86% of two-week test-retest consistency and reduce racial and ideological accuracy gaps relative to demographics-only agents (Park et al., 2024). On the other, fine-tuned models improve mainly by learning annotator-specific patterns, not demographic ones (Orlikowski et al., 2025), and sociodemographic prompting alone does not reliably improve subjective judgment (Sun et al., 2025). Whether sparse demographic conditioning recovers within-country differentiation, and how this varies across countries, remains open.
The cultural-bias and individual-level literatures also imply competing cross-country expectations. If unequal country representation is the common constraint, countries with stronger aggregate recovery should also show stronger individual recovery (Durmus et al., 2023; Qu & Wang, 2024; Pataranutaporn et al., 2025). Conversely, aggregation itself predicts divergence: idiosyncratic errors cancel in country means, so aggregate recovery can rest on group-level regularities that individual prediction cannot use (Robinson, 1950). Structural inconsistencies across marginal, multivariate, and subgroup statistics point the same way (Li et al., 2025; Williams et al., 2026). The reviewed literature does not test whether aggregate and individual recovery covary across countries using matched respondents, items, and prompts. RQ3 asks all three together, because the two levels have to be told apart before either can be used:
RQ3. How much individual-level recovery is there, does first- versus third-person framing change it, and does it track aggregate recovery across countries?
3 Data and Method
3.1 Survey benchmark
The benchmark is the European Social Survey (ESS), a cross-national probability-based survey (European Social Survey European Research Infrastructure Consortium, 2026). This paper uses data from ESS Round 11, which covers 30 countries and roughly 50,000 respondents. The analysis includes 42 survey items spanning 12 domains: social trust, institutional trust, political attitudes, political efficacy, subjective well-being, immigration attitudes, social values, national attachment, health, religion, safety, and income. The full item list with question text, scales, and domain classification is reported in Appendix Table A1. Figure 1 below illustrates the simulation pipeline.

3.2 Research design
The experiment is a 2 (model: Qwen, Llama) × 2 (prompt: first person, third person) crossing. Two open-weight instruction-tuned models are tested: Qwen 2.5-7B-Instruct (Alibaba) and Llama 3.1-8B-Instruct (Meta). Both are run locally on a university high-performance computing cluster (NVIDIA A100) using vLLM for batched inference. Temperature is set to 0.7, and respondent subsampling uses a fixed seed of 888.
Two prompt conditions are tested. In the first-person (1P) condition, the model is instructed to adopt the respondent’s persona and answer directly. In the third-person (3P) condition, the model is asked to predict how the described person would respond. Both prompts include the demographic backstory, the item text, the response scale, and a constraint to output a single number. The three social trust items, on whether most people can be trusted, try to be fair, and are helpful, carry the full ESS question wording, which embeds verbal endpoint descriptions. Every other item is presented with the abbreviated codebook wording reported in Table A1. Prompts specify the numeric scale range as digits (e.g., “Scale: 0-10”) and name no scale endpoints in words.
For each country, 685 respondents are randomly subsampled from the ESS. That is the number available in the smallest country sample, so it is the largest subsample an equal-sized design can draw, and the size is set by the data and not by a computational limit. The subsample is drawn with no design weights. The human benchmark is weighted by the ESS post-stratification weight (pspwght) throughout, and the sensitivity of the results to that choice is reported in Section 4.4. The sample size is sufficient at both levels of analysis. Two random halves of the human sample agree at a median between-country correlation of 0.98, so the quantity being estimated is itself nearly noiseless. At n = 685 the median country-mean reliability is 0.99 on the human side and 0.94 on the silicon side, attenuating the between-country correlation by a median factor of 0.97. A single country-item cell has 80% power to detect a within-country correlation of 0.11, and pooling cells lowers it to 0.02, so the near-zero individual recovery in Section 4.3 is a measured near-zero.
Each respondent is prompted for all 42 items, yielding 28,770 prompts per country and 863,100 per model-prompt condition. Across the four model and prompt combinations the main experiment comprises 3,452,400 response attempts, and 33,743,100 across all 41 arms including the backstory, ablation, probe, and scale arms described below. The human-model valid intersection covered 97.74% of scheduled responses for Qwen 1P, 97.80% for Qwen 3P, 97.81% for Llama 1P, and 75.71% for Llama 3P. The Llama 3P shortfall is not uniform across items and is diagnosed in Section 4.4. The loss falls on the wide response scales, so that condition cannot identify a framing effect.
All four model-prompt conditions are reported for aggregate recovery and individual-level means (Figures 2 and 6). Detailed domain and model-prompt comparisons appear in the Appendix. The backstory, ablation, and scale experiments use Qwen 1P, because their purpose is source decomposition and not another model comparison. The country-label pair is also replicated under Llama (Section 4.2). Generation is unseeded, so one arm was run twice to establish a noise floor.
3.3 Backstory construction
Each respondent is described using a natural-language backstory generated from 20 ESS demographic variables: country, gender, age, birth year, marital status, domicile type, region, birth country, citizenship, education level, education years, main activity, household income decile, left-right political orientation, party affiliation, household size, children at home, trade union membership, discrimination group membership, and internet use frequency. These are core sociodemographic measures that any probability survey collects, so a backstory built from them is available wherever silicon sampling might be applied. The set was fixed for that scalability, and alternative variable sets were not explored. What each block of the fixed set contributes is measured in Section 3.4. The choice also gives the evaluation practical force: if conditioning on these standard variables fails, silicon sampling has little to offer the representative cross-national surveys it is meant to replace. An initial design used 27 backstory variables, seven of which overlapped with the 42 outcome items and created potential information leakage. Removing them left the 20 backstory variables above and reduced the inflated recovery (Appendix Table A2).
All backstories, prompts, and question texts are in English. Each backstory is constructed by mapping ESS codes to natural-language sentences with fixed value-label dictionaries (e.g., gender code 2 becomes “I am female”). Country codes from the International Organization for Standardization (ISO) are mapped to English country names (e.g., code DE becomes “I live in Germany”). Party affiliation is represented generically. No country-specific party is named. This generic coding omits a predictor of political attitudes and may reduce individual-level recovery. The sentences form one paragraph, using the same fixed mapping across countries.
An abbreviated example prompt is provided in Appendix B. The 3P prompt replaces its first line with “Consider a person with the following background:” and rephrases the question as “how would this person respond?” Because prompting language affects cultural outputs (Lu et al., 2025; Tao et al., 2024), the English-only design may favor recovery of English-speaking opinion patterns. The replication materials provide the analysis-ready model summaries, the analysis and figure code, and the raw ESS benchmark.
3.4 Backstory conditioning experiment
The backstory conditioning experiment asks whether aggregate recovery comes from the country label or from compositional differences across national samples, using Qwen 1P throughout. The design is a set of paired contrasts on a common rich profile, each observed with and without one block on the same respondents and items. One pair differs only by the country sentence (both of its arms omit the region code, whose prefix would re-encode the country), one only by the region code, and one only by the political-identity variables.
The same with-and-without logic runs over the whole profile. The 20 backstory variables partition into 10 named blocks: the base (gender, age, birth year), the country label, the region code, political identity, education with main activity and income, union membership with internet use, household composition, migration and citizenship, minority status, and domicile. The political block holds the same two variables the political ablation removes, so the chapter has one definition of it.
The unit is the block because single variables often repeat one another. The profile contains near-duplicate pairs: age and birth year, education level and education years, household size and children at home. Age and birth year encode the same fact, so dropping one leaves its twin in place and the contrast measures the repetition. Where a block holds no such pair, its variables are also tested one at a time.
Every block is observed at the two margins that bound its contribution. It is added alone to the sparse base plus the country label, and it is removed alone from the otherwise complete profile. These are the extreme positions a block can occupy in any order of additions, so the two margins bound it under every ordering. Three probes settle what they leave open. A country-only arm carries the label with no demographics at all. Two twin arms state the same age as a number of years and as a birth year. Union membership and internet use are added one variable at a time.
A swapped-label arm replaces every backstory’s country sentence with a wrong country, under a fixed derangement of the 30 countries, and leaves the rest of the profile intact. The logic is that of a placebo test (Eggers et al., 2024). If the label works as a group-level prior, recovery should follow the false label and abandon the respondents’ real countries. If aggregate recovery came from the composition of the profiles, mislabeling should not move it. The arm tests whether the model’s sensitivity to the country cue is specific to the country named. Nationality-assigned personas have been manipulated before (Kamruzzaman & Kim, 2024). Scoring swapped labels against the named country’s measured survey means has no direct precedent.
A 2 (country label: present, absent) × 2 (response scale: numbers only, numbers with verbal endpoints) experiment tests whether the format decides the direction of the label’s effect. Its four arms run on a 22-item batch: 12 of the 13 reverse-coded items (vote is excluded, because its response options carry no scale direction), six forward-coded controls (items whose numeric scale runs in the intuitive direction), and four placebo items whose endpoints the question text already describes. The reverse-item set and the two exclusions were fixed in advance, which leaves 10 forward-coded and 10 reverse-coded items in the analysis.
3.5 Evaluation metrics
Recovery denotes the correspondence between model-generated and human survey responses, at the level of aggregation a question concerns: country means for aggregate recovery, respondents within a country for individual recovery. It is assessed along three dimensions: rank-order correspondence, distributional spread, and mean-level bias. Pearson correlations are used for continuity with the silicon-sampling literature (Argyle et al., 2023; Bisbee et al., 2024).
The argument rests on two correlations. The primary aggregate measure, rbc, is the per-item correlation across the 30 country means. The primary individual measure, rwc, is the within-country correlation across respondents, computed per country-item cell. Three further quantities are diagnostic and appear only where a question requires them. Each country’s 42-item aggregate profile correlation, rprof, carries the country-level comparison in Section 4.1. Two pooled quantities appear once each and carry no symbol. A pooled within-country correlation, taken across respondents from every country after centering within country, is used in Figure 6(b), where its statistical power suits the per-item display. A pooled correlation across all 1,260 country-item cells is reported as a labeled counterexample, because pooling lets scale differences between items read as cross-national signal. The countries are the full ESS set and the items a fixed battery, so these correlations estimate no wider parameter and carry no interval. Uncertainty is reported where it exists, in the sampling error inside each country mean and in the run-to-run variation of Section 4.4. Definitions appear in Appendix D.
Three reference points make these correlations interpretable. Two identical unseeded runs differ by a median absolute 0.060 in rbc per item, which sets a noise floor. Regressing each human response on five demographic predictors with country fixed effects gives a median within-country multiple correlation of 0.210. That is the demographic ceiling: the most any demographic profile could recover at the individual level, whatever produces the answers. The third fixes what counts as adequate at the aggregate level: recovery is judged against predictors that use no model at all, built from regional neighbors and macro covariates and reported in Section 4.1. A model that does not beat them adds nothing a researcher could not already compute.
Distributional spread is evaluated by the ratio of model to human standard deviations for each country-item pair, and mean-level bias by the model-minus-human difference. Because a correlation is invariant to location and scale, Lin’s concordance coefficient is also reported. It factors into rbc and an accuracy term, separating rank correspondence from calibration. Model and prompt contrasts use paired t-tests across the 42 items.
4 Results
4.1 Silicon sampling’s aggregate recovery is moderate and item-dependent
Addressing RQ1, aggregate recovery is moderate, uneven across items, and not captured by any single measure. Country-profile and mean-error statistics produce different country rankings, and the pooled correlation is a different quantity again. Figure 2(a) shows every item in every condition, with the pooled correlation drawn once as a labeled counterexample. Under Qwen 1P it is 0.822 while the median item-level correlation is 0.443. The 0.379 gap carries no additional cross-national signal. Pooling places a 0-to-10 trust item near 5 and a 1-to-4 attitude item near 2 in one scatter, so most of the covariance it captures lies between items. Item means carry 96.5% of the pooled covariance, and removing each item’s mean leaves a pooled correlation of 0.515.
Against the pre-declared thresholds, 20 of 42 items exceed 0.5 and nine exceed 0.7. Qwen 1P performs best of the four conditions and is the reference for the focused analyses below, and all four appear in Figures 2 and 6, so the choice does not affect the main conclusions.

Trust items dominate the top (trust in the police 0.82, trust in parliament 0.79, trust in the legal system 0.78). Ranks beyond the top two are not reported. They exchange places between independent runs of the same condition, as the noise floor of 0.060 per item implies.
Of the 11 inversions, 10 are reverse-coded items, and direction alone explains 66% of the variance in rbc across the 42 items. Among forward-coded items, recovery also rises with the size of the true cross-national differences (r = 0.46, p = 0.012 across the 29 items). One inversion is not explained by direction. Whether European unification should go further (−0.23) is forward-coded. It is a bipolar item whose numeric direction is a convention. In Figure 2(b) it is the one circle in the negative tail. Appendix Figure A2 puts a recovered item and an inverted one side by side. Section 4.2 tests the scale-direction account experimentally.
Recovery also varies across country profiles. For each country, rprof is the correlation between the 42 human and model means. Figure 7 ranks all 30 of them. Under Qwen 1P, rprof ranges from 0.69 in Bulgaria to 0.91 in Finland (mean 0.82, standard deviation 0.06). Finland, Switzerland, Ireland, Norway, and Austria rank highest (0.88–0.91), while Bulgaria, Serbia, Ukraine, Israel, and Latvia rank lowest (0.69–0.76). The band is narrow, so the measure does not discriminate well enough to select countries to trust.
The pattern depends on the error metric. After rescaling each item to the unit interval, mean absolute error averages 0.195 and ranges only from 0.16 to 0.22, and several Western European countries move into the higher-error group. Country ordering is nevertheless similar across Qwen and Llama (r = 0.76 for normalized error, 0.97 for rprof), so the same countries are easier or harder for both models even though the regional gradient is weak.
Rank correspondence and calibration are close to independent. All 30 countries have positive mean bias under Qwen 1P, from 0.36 scale points in Finland to 1.40 in Bulgaria, consistent with a shift toward higher numeric responses. Mean standard-deviation ratios range from 0.54 in Estonia to 0.68 in Portugal with no clear geographic gradient. Lin’s concordance coefficient separates ranking from calibration and shows that 87% of the agreement attainable at the observed rank correspondence is lost to bias and compression (Appendix F). A reader who wants to rank countries gets moderate performance. A reader who wants to use the number as an estimate of a country’s value gets almost nothing.
Predictors that use no model at all recover the same country means better. One predicts each country’s item mean by the leave-one-out average of its regional neighbors. It gives a median rbc of 0.55 against the model’s 0.44 and a median normalized error of 0.056 against 0.180, and it wins on level accuracy for 41 of 42 items. Adding log GDP per capita to region dummies raises the comparator to 0.62 and 0.052. Section 5.3 takes up what that means for a user.
4.2 The country label carries the signal, the response format its direction
Addressing RQ2, the swapped-label arm shows that the model reads the country name it is given. Scored against the respondents’ own country, the swapped arm reaches a forward-item median rbc of 0.34, against 0.40 for the same profile with no country name at all. Per item the paired difference is −0.096, with a bootstrap interval over items of [−0.142, −0.003]. A wrong name costs the respondents’ own country more than no name does. A cue the model attends to should do that. A cue it ignores could not. The arm does not, however, hand recovery to the country it names. Scored against that country it reaches 0.40, well short of the 0.56 the true name reaches, and the paired difference between the two scorings is +0.012 [−0.068, +0.122]. The label is used without taking over, and the rest of the profile still carries country information of its own. The two scorings are not mechanically coupled: under the derangement the named and the true countries’ human means correlate at a median of −0.07. Figure 3 puts the conditions side by side.

Removing the country sentence moves recovery more than removing anything else does. It is taken out of an otherwise identical rich profile, holding the same respondents and items. With the country present the paired item-level correlation is higher by a median 0.135 on forward-coded items, where 27 of 29 improve (p < 0.001), and lower by 0.176 on reverse-coded items, where 1 of 13 improves. Of the three original removal contrasts, only the country label produces an effect that survives correction for multiplicity and exceeds the noise floor (Appendix H).
No other block comes near the country label. Adding the country name to a backstory that already specifies gender, age, and birth year raises median rbc from −0.03 to 0.52 (Fisher z +0.73 on forward items, −0.44 on reverse items), and nothing else approaches either of its margins. Figure 4 gives every block’s effect at both margins, and Appendix Figure A3 gives the country-label contrast item by item. Union membership and internet use is the only other block beyond the noise floor on both (+0.09 added, +0.08 removed, forward items). Added one variable at a time, that gain comes from internet use (+0.13 against union’s +0.06), itself a strong country gradient.

The blocks that describe the respondent subtract. Political identity lowers recovery when added to the sparse base (−0.12) and does nothing when removed from the full profile. The socioeconomic block lowers it at both margins (−0.13 added, −0.06 removed), and removing household income alone reproduces nearly the whole block effect (−0.055 of −0.061). The margins are also not additive: political identity and the socioeconomic block each subtract on their own, yet the full profile sits at 0.44. Block effects do not accumulate. The label’s effect is also larger when it is added than when it is removed (+0.73 against +0.20), because the full profile already carries some country information in the composition of its other variables, which Section 5.4 bounds.
The added information hurts even where it is directly relevant. Household income decile enters with the economic block, and income items recover worse with it than without. Dispersion sharpens the point: even in the best condition the model reproduces only a fraction of the true cross-national spread, and the between-country dispersion ratio is highest at the two richest profiles, where the correlation sits below the country-only level. Enrichment widens cross-national differentiation and misdirects it.
The label needs no demographics at all. It recovers 0.52 without them, the same as with them, and the full profile recovers less than the label on its own (0.44). Stating the same age as a birth year leaves recovery unchanged, so the step does not depend on the surface form of the base. The gain is confined to the aggregate. Median within-country recovery remains near zero in every arm, so the manipulation that produces the cross-national correspondence yields no improvement in individual prediction.
The country-label contrast replicates in the second model. Under Llama 1P the same removal moves forward items by +0.25 and reverse items by −0.20, with 25 of 29 forward items improving. Taken together the two margins and the swapped-label arm describe a group-level prior: the label places countries against one another, the rest of the profile does not refine that placement, and replacing the name degrades the placement it was carrying.
One explanation covers both the inversions of Section 4.1 and the negative label effect above, and the 2 × 2 experiment tests it (Figure 5). The model may be reading the numeric scale in the wrong direction while holding correct country associations. Under numeric scales the country label helps forward-coded items (mean Fisher-z effect +0.15) and harms reverse-coded ones (−0.24, 1 of 10 improving). Under anchored scales it helps both (+0.22 and +0.24, 10 of 10 and 8 of 10 improving). The triple difference is the change in the country effect on reverse items minus the change on forward items. It is +0.42, with a 95% bootstrap interval of [0.13, 0.79]. The forward-reverse gap closes from +0.39 to −0.03, which is within the noise floor of zero. Dropping the two largest movers leaves +0.16 [0.05, 0.27]. The result does not depend on them.
The country label therefore supplies cross-national signal, and the response format decides whether the countries come out in the right order or backwards. These are one interacting mechanism, not two parallel ones, and the inversions follow from the format and not from the model’s country knowledge.

4.3 Individual-level recovery is limited and unrelated to the aggregate
Addressing RQ3, recovery remains near zero across model-prompt conditions, and the models reproduce little of the demographic signal available in the human data. Median within-country recovery ranges from 0.006 (Llama 3P) to 0.028 (Qwen 1P), and even the largest implies less than 0.1% shared variance. First- and third-person framing do not differ in either model (Table A4). With 20,550 respondents per condition many of these correlations are statistically significant and substantively negligible. That weakness is not an absence of demographic signal in the human benchmark: the five-predictor ceiling is 0.210 against an achieved 0.028, so the model recovers 13.5% of what a demographic profile could in principle deliver. Figure 6 shows both cuts of the individual level: every condition against that ceiling, and the same 42 items on the scale of Figure 2(b).

Individual recovery is weak in every country. It is computed for each of the 1,260 country-item cells. Under Qwen 1P, mean rwc is 0.027, with a standard deviation of 0.079. Of these, 826 are positive and 453 reach p < 0.05. Even Israel, the highest country mean at 0.077, has less than 1% of individual variance explained. In a replicate arm 95% of the within-country variance the model produces is generation noise. No country reaches a level that supports substantive individual inference.
Recovery varies more by item than by country. Life satisfaction (0.146) and happiness (0.142) recover best, whereas feeling about household income (−0.148) and whether gay men and lesbians should be free to live as they wish (−0.085) are consistently negative. At this level 11 of the 42 items are negative, the same count as at the aggregate level and largely the same items (Appendix Figure A4, and Table A3 by domain). That is the pattern a direction failure operating at both levels would produce. What is asked therefore matters more than which country is simulated.
Anchoring is the one manipulation that moves this level (Section 4.2). It raises median within-country recovery from 0.028 to 0.079, or from 13.5 to 31.9% of the demographic ceiling. Individual recovery is negligible under numeric scales and roughly triples when the scale direction is made explicit. It stays far below the ceiling in both cases.
Dispersion fails the pre-declared band in both directions. Under Qwen 1P the mean ratio of model to human standard deviations is 0.61, using the mean of the per-cell ratios. The median is 0.57 and the ratio of means 0.59, and all three fail the pre-declared 0.9–1.1 band. Llama 3P instead expands variance (1.41). Aggregate rank correspondence therefore does not imply distributional recovery.
The two levels sit far apart. The pooled correlation lies between 0.64 and 0.83 across conditions while within-country recovery is near zero (Figures 2 and 6). Figure 6(b) draws the individual level on the axis of Figure 2(b), so the two series are directly comparable: the aggregate spans 1.64 correlation units against the individual 0.30, a ratio of 5.5. The aggregate-individual dissociation is established for single-country designs. What is new is that it survives across 30 countries and that scale anchoring moves both levels together.
The two levels do not share a geographic pattern either. Across 30 countries, rprof and rwc correlate at −0.21, with a 90% confidence interval of [−0.49, 0.10]. Using range-normalized error in place of rprof gives +0.07. With 30 countries the data cannot distinguish a null from a moderate association, so the result is a bound. A scatter of the two quantities would draw that uncertainty as noise, so Figure 7 shows the decision-relevant object instead: the two country orderings side by side, joined per country.
The decision this bears on is pairwise, so counting is better than correlating: of the 435 country pairs, 232 are ordered one way by aggregate profile recovery and the other way by individual recovery. A researcher choosing between two countries on aggregate evidence would therefore be wrong slightly more often than right. Switzerland against Israel is the sharpest case: profile correlations of 0.91 and 0.75 but within-country correlations of 0.014 and 0.077. The aggregate-individual correlation is also non-positive under Qwen 3P (−0.52), Llama 1P (−0.21), and Llama 3P (−0.22). Aggregate performance cannot be used to select countries for individual-level work, and RQ3 finds no evidence that stronger aggregate recovery predicts stronger individual recovery.

4.4 Robustness checks
The 16 checks leave the conclusions where they stand, with one exception: parse coverage in the Llama third-person arm. They vary the human benchmark, the investigator codings, the estimator and the aggregator, the comparator’s specification, parse coverage, and the run and the round. Appendix Table A5 gives the quantity each one moves. Two of them set the terms on which the rest are read, and both are given here.
The direction coding was fixed in advance and a second model reproduces it. It comes from the ESS codebook and was set before any result was seen, and its agreement with an independently trained model is r = 0.84.
Run-to-run stability sets the band an effect has to clear. The median absolute difference in rbc between the two identical runs is 0.060 per item, with a maximum of 0.185. Every effect interpreted in this paper exceeds that band. A second replicate, run on the full battery under a different generation seed, gives a smaller median of 0.029 and a larger maximum of 0.242. Of the eight items beyond the band, the two largest are items the dispersion screen already declares uninterpretable. The band is kept at 0.060, which is the conservative choice.
Parse coverage in the Llama third-person arm is the exception. Restricting to items above 80% coverage moves the median rbc from +0.17 to −0.00. The correlation falls as the threshold tightens, so the usable subset is biased and not cleaner: the poorly covered items are the wide response scales, on which recovery is otherwise strongest. Better parsing would not rescue the condition. Re-parsing every rejected response under four alternative extraction rules moves the median rbc in that arm by at most 0.013, against a run-to-run noise floor of 0.060 (Appendix Table A6). The framing null rests on Qwen alone.
5 Discussion and Conclusion
Read separately, the three findings look like three unrelated failures. The scale experiment shows they are not. Naming the two scale endpoints in words removes the item inversions, reverses the sign of the country-label effect on reverse-coded items, and triples individual-level recovery. One manipulation does all three. Two of the three main results therefore share a cause, and the cause lies in the measurement instrument. The country label result stands apart: the label, and not the profile around it, carries the aggregate signal.
5.1 The country label acts as a group-level prior
The country label carries the aggregate signal, and richer profiles do not improve on it (Section 4.2). A model that found respondent detail uninformative would show flat margins. The margins are negative, so the detail is used, and used badly. It is used as noise. Almost none of the variation the model produces within a country is respondent-specific, and nearly all of it is generation noise (Section 4.3). The label is also what steers: replacing it with a wrong name costs the country the respondents came from.
The model orders countries without calibrating the distances between them. Calibrated country knowledge would reproduce the spacing and the level of the country means, not only their order. The model does neither. It compresses cross-national spread, it shifts every country’s mean in the same direction, and most of the agreement available at the observed ranking is lost to that displacement (Section 4.1 and Appendix F).
Two other readings do not survive. Identifiability is not the mechanism. If it were, recovery would track it, and recovery instead varies widely among profiles that identify the country equally well, with the sparsest profile containing the country beating every richer one (Appendix I). Sensible cue use is not the mechanism either. Country may be the most informative single cue for these outcomes, so leaning on it could be cue use and not stereotype activation, and enrichment separates the two readings. A model weighing cues on their validity would not lose accuracy when given more valid ones, and the socioeconomic block widens cross-national differentiation while lowering the correlation (Section 4.2).
A model can tell countries apart without representing any of them, as human national-character beliefs can (Terracciano et al., 2005). LLM population simulations show uneven correspondence across countries (Durmus et al., 2023; Qu & Wang, 2024). The observed correspondence cannot distinguish survey knowledge from online discourse, national stereotypes, or response-scale priors. The country label should therefore be read as a strong aggregate prior, not as evidence that the model reconstructs the population it names.
The label result speaks to two literatures at once. Cultural-alignment research asks which national profiles models resemble (Durmus et al., 2023; Tao et al., 2024). Survey-simulation research asks whether conditioned outputs reproduce the target population (Bail, 2024; Bisbee et al., 2024). A country label delivers the first without the second.
5.2 Item-level failure is a property of the instrument
The inversions are an instrument effect: the anchoring experiment of Section 4.2 removes them while holding the model, the respondents, and the items fixed. Prompt format is therefore part of the measurement instrument, and the instrument here was minimal by design, giving the model a bare numeric range where an ESS respondent hears the full question and sees a labeled show card. Röttger et al. (2024) reach the same conclusion for the measured political position of models under different question formats. What the numeric arm measures is a compound of the model’s country associations and its reading of the scale, and only the anchored arm separates the two. This is a measurement-equivalence problem of the kind cross-national survey research already recognizes between human populations (King et al., 2004; Davidov et al., 2014), arising here between human and model. A validation has to report the response format it used, because the same model and the same items rank countries the other way round under another one.
Most of the variance in item-level recovery follows direction, and what is left has a pattern of its own. The items that recover are about the country, and the ones that invert (income, health, safety) are about the respondent. One reading, formed after the results, is that the model recovers what is publicly said about a country and not what its residents report about themselves. Institutional trust and subjective well-being have a large descriptive literature and track visible national conditions (Norris, 2011; Diener et al., 1995), while how safe a respondent feels walking home after dark has no comparable public ordering. The reading fits the prior of Section 5.1 and predicts which items a country cue can carry, and recovery is larger where the true cross-national differences are larger (Section 4.1).
High pooled correlations mask negative item-level country correlations by combining well-recovered and inverted items. In these data, high pooled and profile correlations coexist with the reversed items, so a screening rule based on either would pass the battery unchanged. Item-level diagnostics are therefore necessary before pooled or profile metrics are used.
5.3 Aggregate recovery supports rank comparison, not individual or distributional inference
The dissociation between the two levels is the ecological-inference problem in a new setting: relationships among group means need not reproduce relationships among individuals (Robinson, 1950; Firebaugh, 1978), and recovering individual relationships from aggregate data remains a distinct and difficult inferential problem (King, 1997). Silicon sampling reproduces that structure exactly. Evidence for aggregate recovery is therefore not weak evidence for individual recovery. It is evidence for a different claim. Recent evaluations point the same way. LLM-generated data can match marginal statistics while failing multivariate and subgroup structure (Boelaert et al., 2025; Li et al., 2025; Williams et al., 2026). LLM stand-ins reproduce group-level discourse while flattening the within-group variation on which individual recovery depends (Kozlowski & Evans, 2025).
What would close the gap is respondent information, and the amounts involved are large. Higher individual recovery appears only when models receive interview- or annotator-specific data far richer than demographic profiles (Park et al., 2024; Orlikowski et al., 2025), and demographic prompting on its own does not close the gap (Sun et al., 2025; Gao et al., 2025). The profiles here are the scalable demographic minimum by design (Section 3.3), so the absence of improvement measures what that minimum buys. The framing null follows from the same prior account: a model that ignores the mapping from profile to attitude has no reason to answer differently when speaking as the person than when predicting the person. Presentation is the third constraint and the cheapest to change, and Section 5.2 shows it moves both levels at once.
The pattern is about items, not about which countries are well represented. If better-represented populations were easier to simulate, the countries recovered best at the aggregate level would also be recovered best at the individual level (Durmus et al., 2023; Pataranutaporn et al., 2025). They are not, and recovery varies more across items than across countries, which points to a weak mapping from profiles to attitudes, not to a coverage gradient.
Whether recovery is adequate depends on the alternative, and the alternatives are cheap. Both comparators in Section 4.1 require survey data from other countries. The question they pose is whether a researcher who already holds such data should use the model to extend it. On these items, at this sample size, the answer is no.
After item-level validation the model supports exploratory cross-national rank comparison on topics it recovers well, where no survey data for the comparison countries exist. It does not support individual prediction, within-country heterogeneity estimation, or distributional analysis: matching country means can coexist with compressed variance, unstable coefficients, and incorrect dependence structure (Bisbee et al., 2024; Williams et al., 2026; Xie et al., 2026). Aggregate recovery is evidence about a specific estimand, not a certificate of representativeness. Validation should match the intended estimand and the design, since response coding, backstory wording, model choice, and sampling settings all change synthetic distributions (Bisbee et al., 2024; Dominguez-Olmedo et al., 2024; Röttger et al., 2024).
5.4 Contributions, limitations, and future directions
The three contributions are empirical, methodological, and practical. The empirical one is the evaluation itself: item rankings, individual recovery, and the label’s incremental contribution, measured for the first time on the same respondents, items, and prompts. What it finds is a group-level prior: the label carries the aggregate signal, no richer profile improves on it, and none of it reaches the individual level. The methodological one is to make those readings testable. Each block is observed at both of the margins that bound it, so no ordering of additions can be chosen after the fact. A swapped-label arm tests whether the cue is specific to the country named, and a 2 × 2 turns the item inversions from an interpretation into a result. Recovery is reported item by item against a measured run-to-run floor. A pooled correlation would have hidden the inverted items. The practical one is a standard of adequacy. Recovery is judged against model-free comparators a researcher who holds survey data already has, and on these items the regional average wins.
Five limitations define the scope of these conclusions. First, English prompts and generic party coding omit country-specific language and party cues. Second, one European survey program and two open-weight 7–8B models limit generalization to non-European populations, other survey designs, and larger or proprietary models. Third, the no-country arm still carries country information in the composition of harmonized categories, where a classifier recovers the country 28.7% of the time against a 3.3% baseline. Its 0.34 median recovery therefore bounds what composition achieves without a country name, and the arm is not a zero-information control. Fourth, the scale experiment uses Qwen 1P only, and no experiment here separates calibrated country knowledge from stereotype activation. Fifth, the Llama third-person arm lost 24% of scheduled responses to a format-compliance failure, which bounds what that arm can show (Appendix M).
Each limitation names its next step. A factorial design can vary prompt language, country-specific party cues, verbal scale anchors, and profile content independently. The benchmark should extend to non-European surveys and to larger or proprietary models. Compositional country information should be measured and reported wherever a design cannot remove it. The anchoring experiment should be repeated in a second model, and unparsable outputs reported by item and country instead of as one coverage figure. Repeated runs of the same arm would separate item-level signal from generation noise directly. This design has one replicate pair. Two quantities a user needs are still unmeasured here, multivariate structure and subgroup calibration. Each should be judged against the estimand it is meant to serve.

Appendix D. Evaluation metrics
(1) The primary aggregate metric correlates the 30 country means within each item. (2) The pooled metric combines all 1,260 country-item cells and is descriptive only. (3) Pooled individual-level recovery is summarized by a correlation across all respondents. Because pooling respondents combines within-country correspondence with between-country covariance, it is not a pure measure of individual-level prediction. (4) The per-country rwc measure directly evaluates within-country differentiation, while (5) rprof summarizes how well each country’s 42-item aggregate profile is recovered.

Appendix F. Concordance
Pearson’s r is invariant to location and scale, so a model that ranks countries perfectly while compressing their spread and shifting their level still scores 1. Lin’s concordance coefficient rewards agreement with the 45-degree line and factors as rbc × Cb, where Cb is an accuracy term bounded by 1. Under Qwen 1P the median item has rbc = 0.443, Cb = 0.132 and concordance 0.036: 87% of the attainable agreement is lost to calibration, not to ranking. The median absolute bias is 1.13 scale points and the median between-country dispersion ratio 0.28. Of the 42 items, 11 have negative concordance, which is worse than no agreement at all.

Appendix I. Country identifiability
A multinomial classifier was trained to recover the country from the rendered backstory text of each of the 10 variants, with a majority-class baseline of 3.33%. Eight of them reach exactly 100%, because each renders the country sentence, so identifiability is saturated and cannot produce a dose-response. At that identical level, median rbc still ranges from 0.287 for the political variant to 0.523 for the four-variable minimal profile. The variant that omits the country sentence reaches 28.72%, replicated at 28.99% in an independent run. Its strongest n-grams are fragments of harmonized education, marital status, and main activity, and no rendered backstory names a country, so the residual identifiability is compositional.

Appendix M. Parse failures
Of the 863,100 responses scheduled in the Llama third-person arm, 77.28% parsed. Of the remainder, 5.75% fall outside the declared scale, 8.25% carry a value above 1,000, and 8.71% contain no digit. Four explanations were tested and rejected. Truncation: failed outputs have a median length of 61 characters against a maximum of 317, and none falls within 2% of that maximum. Overshoot: 93.5% of out-of-range values lie above the top of the scale, but the median excess is 39 scale points, 6.7 times the scale width. Scale confusion: the out-of-range values take 2,963 distinct values instead of clustering on values valid elsewhere in the battery. Backstory echo: 2.33% of rejected values match one of that respondent’s own backstory numbers, against 0.94% under a shuffled null. Reading the verbatim outputs shows what does occur: code blocks, enumeration of the scale in place of a choice within it, continuation of the prompt, and a junk number followed by a valid answer. The first-person control parsed 99.99% with 64 rejections, all fractional midpoints, so the difference is the framing, not the model.
Appendix N. Sensitivity to the extraction rule
Every correlation reported here is computed on responses that survived a rule that takes the first number in the response text. That rule discards answers the model gave correctly, such as a junk number followed by a valid one, so all 11.3 million raw responses were re-parsed and every rejected response in the 15 arms landed at the time of the check was re-scored under four alternative rules: the last number in the text, the number following an answer marker, the only in-range value where exactly one exists, and the first in-range value anywhere. The fourth is the loosest and is reported as an upper bound, in the same sense as the leakage bound on the no-country arm. In the Llama third-person arm 22.6% of rejections carry an in-range value somewhere in the text. In every other arm the share is below 38%. No rule moves a median between-country correlation by more than 0.013, and the primary Qwen first-person arm does not move at all under any rule (+0.4433 throughout). The largest movement in any single item anywhere is 0.092, in the demographics-only arm, whose median is near zero under every rule.
